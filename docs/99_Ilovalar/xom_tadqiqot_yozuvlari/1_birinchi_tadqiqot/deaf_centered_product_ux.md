# Deaf-centered communication technology: needs, evidence, product/UX principles (as of Nov 2026)

Scope note: global evidence, preferring 2019-2026 sources. Most empirical studies come from high-income countries (US/UK/EU). WFD reports that about 80% of deaf people live in developing countries, which describes Uzbekistan's situation. Treat every finding as something to validate locally with Uzbek Deaf organisations.

Baseline numbers:
- More than 430 million people have disabling hearing loss, possibly close to 700 million by 2050. Nearly 2.5 billion people (1 in 4) are projected to have some degree of hearing loss by 2050 — [WHO news release, 2021](https://www.who.int/news/item/02-03-2021-who-1-in-4-people-projected-to-have-hearing-problems-by-2050); [WHO WRH launch](https://www.who.int/news-room/events/detail/2021/03/03/default-calendar/launch-of-the-world-report-on-hearing)
- About 60 million people have severe-to-complete hearing loss (figure from the WHO report as quoted by Cochlear) — [Cochlear press release](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/1375922/cochlear-welcomes-landmark-who-report-calling-on-governments-and-societies-to-prioritize-hearing-health)
- About 70 million deaf people worldwide. The WFD homepage says "200+ sign languages", while many secondary and UN-derived sources say more than 300. More than 80% live in developing countries — [WFD homepage](https://wfdeaf.org/?p=56); [UN/Vikaspedia](https://en.vikaspedia.in/viewcontent/social-welfare/differently-abled-welfare/important-days/international-day-of-sign-languages). The count of sign languages differs between these sources.
- Population distinction for product scope: "Deaf" (capital D) means sign-language users with a cultural and linguistic identity. "Hard of hearing" or late-deafened people mostly use spoken or written language and captions. "Non-speaking" people (aphasia, autism, cerebral palsy, ALS, laryngectomy) usually hear but cannot speak, and they need AAC rather than sign or captions. These are three different products.

## 1. Deaf community perspectives on AI sign translation and signing avatars

### Takeaway
The official Deaf position (WFD/WASLI 2018, since reaffirmed) says avatars and AI must not replace qualified human interpreters for live, complex or high-stakes content. Static, pre-recorded information is a possible exception. Deaf-led research (2023-2025) adds that the field is hearing-led and built on unsuitable data, and that Deaf people must lead the work, not just be consulted. No newer joint WFD/WASLI avatar statement after 2018 was found.

### Cited Findings
- The WFD and WASLI joint statement on signing avatars (14 March 2018, updated 14 April 2018) is concerned about how public authorities decide where to use avatars and advises against using avatars in place of human signers, citing the gap in linguistic quality — [WFD/WASLI statement PDF](https://wfdeaf.org/wp-content/uploads/WFD-and-WASLI-Statement-on-Avatar-FINAL-14032018-Updated-14042018.pdf); [WASLI news](https://wasli.org/news/wfd-wasli-statement-use-signing-avatars/)
- The statement says avatars do not match the quality of trained, qualified interpreters and that machine translation has not matched human live interpretation. It is particularly concerned about live, complex or highly important information such as news. It notes that online sign dictionaries lack a full digitised corpus and that avatars only work one way, from spoken or written language to sign. It also acknowledges that 3D avatars could offer new ways to render written or spoken information in sign — [WFD/WASLI statement](https://wfdeaf.org/wp-content/uploads/WFD-and-WASLI-Statement-on-Avatar-FINAL-14032018-Updated-14042018.pdf)
- WASLI re-hosted the statement in July 2023, which suggests it is still current, but no substantive 2023/2024 revision was found — [WASLI 2023 upload](https://wasli.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/WFD-and-WASLI-Statement-on-Avatar-FINAL-14032018-Updated-14042018-1.pdf)
- A WFD/WFDYS submission to the UN children's rights General Comment repeats the caution against avatars as replacements for interpreters, while allowing their use for pre-recorded, static customer information — [WFD & WFDYS input](https://wfdeaf.org/wp-content/uploads/WFD-and-WFDYS-input-to-the-General-Comment-on-children-rights-Final-document.pdf)
- WFD position on Deaf primacy: the principle that deaf people should take part in and lead the development and teaching of national sign languages also applies to new AI tools that use national sign languages — [WFD](https://wfdeaf.org/?p=18327)
- At the WFD Conference 2025, an AI and sign languages workshop gave attendees a checklist for evaluating any sign-language AI project. It covers data ownership, community involvement, transparency and real-world performance — [WFD](https://wfdeaf.org/?p=12027) (the checklist text itself was not retrieved)
- The SAFE-AI Task Force's Advisory Group on AI and Sign Language Interpreting (formed fall 2023, about 50 members from interpreting companies, academia, non-profits, AI developers and the Deaf community) published a #DeafSafeAI report in Feb 2024. Its evidence base is a qualitative discourse analysis of three webinars titled "Are Deaf Communities READY for AI?" — [3Play ACCESS 2024 slides](https://www.3playmedia.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Advocating-for-DeafSafeAI-Regulations-ACCESS-2024.pdf); [Newswire press release](https://www.newswire.com/news/sign-language-interpreting-leaders-chart-a-path-for-ethical-ai-in-sign-22254286)
- The report names three impact areas that need regulation: (1) results and outcomes, (2) technological quality, and (3) readiness, meaning whether society and institutions are prepared. It found a nearly equal emphasis on the social aspects of interpreting and on technical quality. Open questions it raises: who defines baselines, benchmarks and performance standards, and when experimental tools should move into real-world use — [Newswire](https://www.newswire.com/view/content/sign-language-interpreting-leaders-chart-a-path-for-ethical-ai-in-sign-22254286); [CoSET notes, Colorado legislature](https://leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/images/lcs/coset_-_laab_notes.pdf)
- Deaf panelists said AI interpreting cannot be judged by written-language translation standards. They also recalled that VRI was rolled out widely in hospitals without Deaf input or oversight and has been "extremely harmful" — [3Play ACCESS 2024](https://www.3playmedia.com/access/2024-hub/deaf-safe-ai)
- The SAFE-AI Task Force released its "Guidance for the Safe and Ethical Use of AI in Interpreting" on 26 June 2024. It draws on a CSA Research perception survey with more than 9,400 data points and warns that sign-language users, like speakers of other low-resource languages, will be disproportionately affected — [Newswire](https://www.newswire.com/view/content/first-of-its-kind-ethical-ai-guidance-for-interpreting-released-by-22376003)
- The Advisory Group became the Coalition for Sign Language Equity in Technology (CoSET). CoSET published the "AI Interpreting Solutions Evaluation Toolkit Part A" and a risk framework for automated interpreting, presented at the SLxAI Summit (April 2026) — [MultiLingual](https://multilingual.com/coset-safe-ai-task-force-risk-framework-automated-interpreting-slxai-summit/); [NIDG announcement](https://groups.google.com/g/NIDG/c/18CccWT2FJ8)
- Desai et al. (2024), "Systemic Biases in Sign Language AI Research": all five authors are DHH. They reviewed 101 papers from 2021-2023:
  - 64 papers frame their work as fixing "communication barriers", which treats deaf people as deficient.
  - Of 43 public datasets, only 16 use DHH signers exclusively.
  - Three interpreter-only datasets (interpreted TV broadcasts) are used by 41 of 60 continuous-recognition works.
  - 51 papers rely on glosses, and 30 use glosses alone.
  - 34 papers use pose estimators not trained on sign language, which can drop facial grammar.
  - The paper calls for Deaf researchers to lead agenda-setting, with hearing researchers in a support role, plus positionality statements and linguistic transparency.
  - Sources: [arXiv 2403.02563](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.02563v1); [SignLang 2024 workshop](https://aclanthology.org/2024.signlang-1.6.pdf)
- Angelini (CHI 2023 Extended Abstracts): the Deaf community is "often lukewarm" about signing avatars, citing the "systematic underestimation of the complexity entailed in sign languages", poor quality and lack of direct Deaf participation. The paper argues avatars need contexts of use that Deaf people find acceptable — [BibBase / CHI EA 2023, DOI 10.1145/3544549.3583946](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/angelini-contrastingtechnologistsandactivistspositionsonsigningavatars-2023)
- In a German focus group on avatars, facial expressions got the most votes as the most important quality factor, followed by natural body movement, emotions, appearance and comprehensibility. Most avatar researchers are not part of the Deaf community — [Toward an evaluation model for signing avatars (Mada/Nafath 2022)](https://nafath.mada.org.qa/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/5-Toward-an-evaluation-model-for-signing-avatars.pdf)
- The British Deaf Association's AI and BSL discussion paper says successful AI BSL development requires "deep involvement of the deaf signing community" — [BDA](https://bda.org.uk/ai-bsl-discussion-paper/)
- A 2025 paper ("Deaf in AI: AI language technologies and the erosion of linguistic rights") frames AI language tech as a threat to linguistic rights — [arXiv 2505.02519 via fugumt](https://fugumt.com/fugumt/paper_check/2505.02519v1_enmode) (only the title was verified, not its contents)
- Bragg et al. (ASSETS 2019 Best Paper) found that sign language recognition, generation and translation research happens in disciplinary silos, and that Deaf studies must be included to understand the community the technology serves — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=599343)

### Inferences
- For Uzbekistan: an avatar or AI signer from Uzbek text to Uzbek Sign Language could at most be acceptable for static, pre-recorded, low-stakes content (shop hours, bank FAQs, form instructions). Under the WFD position, live hospital, police or court use of AI sign translation would be rejected.
- An app positioned as "replacing interpreters" will likely face organised opposition from the Deaf community and interpreters. "Getting you to a human interpreter faster, cheaper and anywhere" fits the stated community position.
- Uzbek Sign Language almost certainly lacks a large, Deaf-produced, glossed corpus. Building one ethically, with Deaf ownership and pay, is itself a high-value, community-aligned contribution.
- Use the CoSET toolkit and the WFD 2025 checklist as an external review rubric before launching any AI sign feature.

### Gaps
- The full text of the SAFE-AI Advisory Group report (safeaitf.org/deafsafeai) could not be fetched, so its specific criteria are not verified. Only press and slide summaries are available.
- No WFD-specific 2023/2024 AI position paper with a date was found. The WFD 2025 checklist contents were not retrieved.
- No documented Deaf-community critique of a specific product's (e.g. Hand Talk) co-design process was found.

## 2. Tools Deaf people actually use daily, and literacy

### Takeaway
Deaf signers strongly prefer visual and signed communication: video calls in sign, human interpreters and video messages. In practice, though, they use written text a lot because it is fast and frictionless. The cost and bandwidth of video is a real barrier in lower-income markets. Many Deaf adults have limited literacy in the majority written language (the US median for deaf school leavers has been around 4th-grade reading level for decades), so a text-only product excludes a large part of the core audience.

### Cited Findings
- A UW HCI survey of ASL signers found strong preferences for signed and visual communication. Yet 77% of respondents who post on public feeds used written English and 51% used ASL. The main barrier to signing publicly is that recording and uploading video takes too long — [UW HCI Design, Medium](https://medium.com/hci-design-at-uw/social-app-accessibility-for-deaf-signers-9b3f84027c92) (survey date not confirmed)
- A 2023 Frontiers study found that platforms used and time spent differ by communication modality, and that DHH sign-language users use social media less overall than DHH spoken-language users. It cites a German survey where WhatsApp is the preferred messenger for 95% of young respondents — [Frontiers in Communication 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2023.1175461/epub)
- A 2022 qualitative study (5 key informants and 12 deaf community members) found deaf people use WhatsApp/Facebook, including emoticons, to build online interactions and for self-disclosure — [Emoticons as self-disclosure (ITS)](https://scholar.its.ac.id/en/publications/emoticons-as-self-disclosure-in-social-media-and-its-meaning-for-/)
- In South Africa, mobile video uses much more bandwidth than text or voice, so Deaf signers pay more to communicate in their own language — [UWC repository](https://repository.uwc.ac.za/handle/10566/3705)
- A GSMA survey of users of on-demand sign interpretation apps reports high demand and value. It notes interpreter services have historically been expensive and concentrated in urban areas — [GSMA M4D blog](https://www.gsma.com/mobilefordevelopment/blog/making-sign-language-interpretation-accessible-for-all-through-mobile-assistive-technology/) (page returned 403, so the figures come from the search snippet only)
- In US national data, the median literacy of deaf high-school graduates has stayed around 4th-grade level since the early 1900s. Roughly 1 in 5 read at or below 2nd grade, and about 1 in 3 read between 2nd and 4th grade — [IES / Easterbrooks](https://ies.ed.gov/about/external-person/susan-easterbrooks)
- Qi & Mitchell (2012): the median reading level of 18-year-old DHH students was about the same as hearing 9-10-year-olds, a pattern that has changed little in about 40 years — [PMC4280560](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4280560)
- One health-literacy study (Rochester) cautions that its highly educated Deaf sample cannot be generalised, so literacy varies widely by education and access — [URMC NCDHR poster](https://urmcpublic-lb1.urmc.rochester.edu/MediaLibraries/URMCMedia/ncdhr/publications-presentations/documents/BarnettREALMposterNTID20070219_000.pdf)
- A 2026 umbrella review (32 systematic reviews) found that reduced health literacy was the most common individual-level barrier for Deaf patients — [BMC Health Services Research 2026, White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/240360/)

### Inferences
- Design for "video-first, text-supported, icon-assisted". Every text screen needs an icon or picture and, for key flows, a short sign-language video (recorded by Deaf signers).
- Async sign video messages must be one-tap: record, then send immediately with no upload friction, compressed for low bandwidth. Recording and upload time is the documented reason signers fall back to text.
- Data cost matters in Uzbekistan, so offer low-bitrate video modes, an indicator showing data used, and possibly zero-rating partnerships with mobile operators.
- Integrate with, or be reachable from, Telegram and WhatsApp, which people already use, rather than forcing a new social graph.
- Keep written Uzbek and Russian in plain language, short sentences, with reading level tested with Deaf users. Literacy may be lower than in US figures because of Soviet-era oralist schooling, but this is not verified.

### Gaps
- No Central Asia or CIS data on Deaf literacy or tech use was found. The US literacy figures are old and limited to school leavers.
- No large multi-channel 2022-2026 survey covering all daily tools (text, captions, VRI, messenger video, writing on a phone) was found.

## 3. Situational needs (medical, emergency, police/legal, employment, education, family)

### Takeaway
Across domains the evidence points the same way. Access to qualified interpreters, and to the right channel for each situation, matters more than any single technology. VRI and text are fine for routine tasks but are not preferred, and often fail, in critical or stressful settings. Emergency access needs real-time and location-aware text, not just SMS. Police and courts routinely fail to provide interpreters. For deaf children, the decisive factor is early, fully accessible language in the home, and 90-95% of them have hearing parents who mostly don't sign.

### Cited Findings
**Medical**
- In a scoping review of physician-deaf patient communication, patients preferred an in-person professional interpreter, then writing, lip-reading and VRI. Barriers include poor accuracy from non-professional interpreters, masks and poor video quality — [ASHA Evidence Maps summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/5c158713-f4a7-ed11-8143-005056834e2b)
- VRI scoping review (JMIR 2022, 15 studies, all from high-income countries):
  - Deaf signers prefer VRI to text-based services and to lipreading or note-writing, but prefer in-person interpreters to VRI.
  - VRI was preferred for non-critical care but not for critical or stressful situations (Yabe 2020; Preusse 2016).
  - Problems: screen too small (recommended at least 19.5 in / 49.5 cm), connectivity, positioning (not usable lying face-down, not accessible for deaf-blind or low-vision users), poor interpreter quality, and older users struggling with updates.
  - Starting VRI without first telling the patient and getting their agreement was resented, and VRI weakened the patient-provider relationship.
  - Kushalnagar et al. (2019, US national): nearly half had no VRI access in the past 12 months, and among users 59% (n=327) rated quality unsatisfactory against 41% (n=228) satisfactory.
  - Myers et al. (2021): accessible communication was associated with 81% lower odds of communication dissatisfaction.
  - No studies measured clinical outcomes.
  - Source: [JMIR 2022 / PMC9227653](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227653/)
- A 2026 umbrella review (32 systematic reviews from 3,749 records) prioritises expanding interpreter provision and Deaf awareness training for staff, and notes that few reviews show the effectiveness of interventions — [BMC HSR 2026](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/240360/8/s12913-026-14444-y.pdf)
- A 2025 narrative systematic review (22 studies) found two themes: challenges in the interpreter's role, and the need for cultural competence — [Wiley HSC 2025](https://onlinelibrary-wiley-com-443.webvpn.zafu.edu.cn/doi/10.1155/hsc/8867224)
- JMIR Rehabilitation 2026 pilot: a custom clinical captioning system on Google STT was tested with 10 DHH adults in mock outpatient visits, using usability ratings and interviews — [JMIR Rehab 2026](https://rehab.jmir.org/2026/1/e79073)

**Emergency**
- The European Accessibility Act (in force 28 June 2025) and the EECC require "functional equivalence" for 112 access. Real-Time Text (RTT) and Total Conversation (video, voice and RTT together) are the named targets. EENA treats SMS and apps as interim solutions — [EENA open letter 2025](https://eena.org/wp-content/uploads/2025_04_23_Open_letter_accessibility_emergency_communications_signed_.pdf); [EDF](https://www.edf-feph.org/publications/open-letter-on-improving-accessibility-in-emergency-communications/)
- Accessible channels (apps, websites, relay) often do not send caller location to the emergency call centre (PSAP) — [EENA open letter](https://eena.org/wp-content/uploads/2025_04_23_Open_letter_accessibility_emergency_communications_signed_.pdf)
- Ireland's RTT pilot concluded that an app-based solution would not meet the equivalence requirement, partly because apps need prior installation and pre-registration — [EENA RTT in Ireland](https://eena.org/document/real-time-text-in-ireland)
- SMS lacks the speed and back-and-forth of real-time text for emergencies — [Accessible.org](https://accessible.org/eaa-emergency-communications/) (vendor source)

**Police/legal**
- A Texas study of 72 signing inmates found only 27.8% were given an interpreter at arrest — [JADARA / NSUWorks](https://nsuworks.nova.edu/jadara/vol36/iss1/4)
- The JUSTISIGNS project (EU) found police often did not know sign users need an interpreter present or what qualifications are required, and deaf people waited many hours for an interpreter — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/when-dealing-with-the-police-deaf-people-are-at-a-major-disadvantage-62027)
- A 2024 study of VRI in Police Scotland custody found responsibility for access was unevenly distributed and interpreter capacity was limited. Police need to look at the whole custody experience, not just "provide an interpreter" — [Heriot-Watt research portal](https://researchportal.hw.ac.uk/en/publications/when-he-comes-into-police-custody-he-has-certain-rights-the-burde/)
- US: Miranda warnings and interrogations of Deaf suspects are routinely interpreted by people without enough skill for clear understanding — [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/384115/summary)
- A Tacoma, WA case: a deaf 911 caller spent 60 hours in jail without an interpreter — [Hearing Health Matters](https://hearinghealthmatters.org/?p=19908)

**Children and family**
- Roughly 90-95% of deaf children are born to hearing parents. At most about 40% of families use sign at home (2010 Gallaudet survey). Up to about 70% of deaf children may be language-deprived (estimate) — [BU 2017](https://www.bu.edu/articles/2017/asl-language-acquisition); [BYU Ballard Brief](https://ballardbrief.byu.edu/issue-briefs/linguistic-neglect-of-deaf-children-in-the-united-states)
- Cochlear implants do not guarantee language outcomes, and parents overwhelmingly do not sign, which creates a risk of language deprivation — [PMC6050834](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6050834)
- Deaf children with early ASL exposure can develop age-appropriate vocabulary in both ASL and spoken English, so sign does not block speech — [JSLHR 2022/2023](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/pdf/10.1044/2022_JSLHR-22-00505?download=true)
- No empirical evidence shows children can overcome language deprivation mainly through interpreted education — [Caselli, Hall & Henner 2020, BU](https://open.bu.edu/bitstream/handle/2144/41846/CaselliHallHenner2020.pdf?sequence=3)
- Evidence on bilingual programmes and literacy is contested. One systematic review found few studies documenting outcomes, which Scott et al. (2021) challenged. A BDA-commissioned 2025 briefing says long-term benefits are hard to show methodologically — [PMC12383669](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12383669); [BDA evidence review 2025](https://bda.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/early-sign-language-benefit-evidence-review-v2.pdf)
- The long-held belief that sign interferes with spoken-language development is contested — [Hall 2017](https://comm4child.ulb.be/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Hall-2017.pdf)

### Inferences
Product mapping for Uzbekistan:
- **Hospital**:
  - One-tap human VRI on a large screen (tablet or kiosk at the reception desk rather than a phone).
  - Prepared symptom and pain cards with sign videos.
  - Ask the patient's consent before starting VRI.
  - Offer to book an in-person interpreter for critical visits (surgery, diagnosis, childbirth).
- **Emergency**:
  - A pre-registered emergency profile that sends location and a standard text such as "I am Deaf, I need text/sign".
  - Pictogram choices for emergency type (fire, medical, police).
  - Integration with Uzbekistan's 112/101/102/103 call centres is the hard part and needs a government partnership. The Irish finding warns that an app alone is not equivalent access.
- **Police/legal**: a card such as "I am Deaf. I need a qualified sign language interpreter. I do not consent to questioning without one." plus a fast route to a vetted legal interpreter. Never offer AI interpretation in legal settings.
- **Bank, shop, taxi**: split-screen text and STT is enough for most short transactions (see section 4). Add pre-made phrase cards (e.g. "card blocked", "address", "change").
- **Family**: a product line for hearing parents of deaf children (sign lessons with Deaf tutors, a baby-sign vocabulary) is a high-impact, under-served segment. Partnering with cochlear-implant and audiology centres to reach parents early may matter more than any translation feature.

### Gaps
- No direct data was found on workplace and employment communication needs, or on employment-gap statistics (that search was not done).
- No evidence from low- and middle-income countries on VRI was found. All 15 studies in the JMIR review come from high-income countries.
- No Uzbekistan-specific data on emergency text access was found.

## 4. Proven service designs (VRI apps, communication cards, split-screen chat, AAC)

### Takeaway
Service models with real deployment and evidence:
- Human on-demand VRI and VRS.
- Live ASR captioning used face to face (Google Live Transcribe-type).
- Symbol-based AAC for non-speaking users. Cboard is open source, offline, in 44+ languages and UNICEF-backed.

Research on captioned conversations shows the hearing partner's behaviour and the ability to correct errors matter as much as ASR accuracy. AAC serves a separate population and should be a distinct mode.

### Cited Findings
- On-demand interpretation apps are highly valued where interpreters are scarce, expensive and urban-concentrated — [GSMA](https://www.gsma.com/mobilefordevelopment/blog/making-sign-language-interpretation-accessible-for-all-through-mobile-assistive-technology/) (search snippet)
- Convo Communications is Deaf-owned and Deaf-run, founded March 2009 by Deaf telecom experts. It provides VRS and interpreting in ASL, BSL and Auslan, calls itself "signing-centric", and is on the Forbes 2026 Accessibility 200 list. It reports 500+ staff in 5 countries, but another listing says about 180, so the figures conflict — [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/convo-communications); [Convo Forbes post](https://www.convo.io/post/convo-on-the-2026-forbes-accessibility-200); [SAGE 2024 article](https://https-sage-cnpereading-com-443.webvpn1.xju.edu.cn/doi/10.1177/23294906241288829)
- Convo also launched a platform that combines a visual notification system with VRS — [Newswire](https://www.newswire.com/news/convo-launches-platform-for-the-deaf-integrating-visual)
- Captioned face-to-face conversation (Seita, Andrew, Huenerfauth, CHI 2020/2021, RIT):
  - In person with Live Transcribe (N=20), the hearing speaker's enunciation and intonation significantly affected Deaf satisfaction.
  - Over Zoom (N=23), eye contact ranked highest.
  - Gesturing and pausing had no significant effect.
  - Source: [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10355406); [a11y review summary](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69e242fa20c3fe3111584d7d)
- CHI 2023 small-group captioning: DHH users wanted non-speech cues (speaker identity, speech rate, volume), partly to give feedback to hearing partners. The Google ASR word error rate was around 9% in earlier analyses — [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10431246)
- Head-worn display ASR study: there is a risk that hearing partners assume ASR is accurate — [CHI captioning PDF](https://jennakangg.github.io/assets/pdfs/ChiCaptioning.pdf)
- EvolveCaptions (2025): hearing partners correct live captions, and a DHH speaker records targeted phrases to fine-tune ASR on deaf speech. With 12 DHH and 6 hearing participants, the word error rate fell for all DHH users within 1 hour — [arXiv 2510.02181](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02181v1)
- Cboard is a free, open-source (GPL-3.0) AAC web/PWA app with symbols and text-to-speech, built for cerebral palsy, intellectual impairment and autism. It works offline, is in about 44 languages (sources vary from 40 to 45+), and was funded by the UNICEF Innovation Fund. In 2020 UNICEF said its effectiveness was still being tested with children, professionals and parents — [UNICEF Innovation](https://www.unicef.org/innovation/stories/giving-every-child-voice-aac-technology); [UNICEF ECARO case study](https://www.unicef.org/eca/media/22051/file/Case%20study%201.pdf); [GitHub cboard-org](https://www.GitHub.com/cboard-org/cboard); [Ace Centre](https://acecentre.org.uk/language-library/cboard-aac)
- Hand Talk (Brazil) uses AI 3D avatars Hugo and Maya for text/speech to Libras or ASL. It added Maya, a Black avatar, after "long research and conversations" on diversity. One user review says its ASL output looks more like Signed Exact English than ASL grammar (anecdotal) — [Hand Talk blog](https://www.handtalk.me/en/blog/how-were-hugo-and-maya-created/); [WhoIsAccessible review](https://whoisaccessible.com/reviews/handtalk-me/)
- Privacy filters in sign data collection: expressive avatars or blurred faces affected willingness to take part, and training on some filtered data can improve recognition — [Bragg et al. ASSETS 2020](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=701029)

### Inferences
- **Split-screen "face-to-face" mode** (phone placed flat on the table: the top half rotated 180 degrees for the hearing person showing live STT, the bottom half for the Deaf person to type or tap phrase cards, which TTS reads aloud):
  - The building blocks are proven; Live Transcribe is widely used.
  - Add a prompt for the hearing person: "speak clearly, look at the person, one at a time". Behaviour drives satisfaction.
  - Show an "ASR may be wrong" cue and let either side tap a word to correct it.
  - Optionally show speaker and volume cues.
- **Uzbek and Russian ASR quality** will be lower than English. Offer Uzbek and Russian with visible confidence levels, and keep a human-interpreter escalation button always visible.
- **Phrase and communication cards**, organised by situation (hospital, pharmacy, bank, taxi, police, shop) and each pairing icon, text and sign video, serve low-literacy users and work offline.
- **AAC mode for non-speaking hearing users** (aphasia, autism, CP) is a separate user journey. Cboard (GPL-3.0) can be forked or localised into Uzbek with symbol sets and Uzbek TTS. Check that GPL obligations fit the business model.
- **Human VRI marketplace**: Uzbekistan's interpreter supply is likely small and concentrated in Tashkent. A remote marketplace with fair pay and scheduling extends reach. This is the GSMA "urban concentration" problem.

### Gaps
- No independent outcome studies for AAC apps (Proloquo2Go, TD Snap, Cboard) in autism, aphasia or CP were retrieved. That search returned only vendor and UNICEF material.
- "Signing Hands" and "Hear2Read" (India) were not verified. Hear2Read is believed to be a screen-reader/TTS project for Indian languages (blind users), not a Deaf product, and this was not confirmed. Do not cite either without checking.
- No published evaluation of a split-screen STT/typing app as one product was found. The evidence is component-level.

## 5. Accessibility guidelines (WCAG, notifications, plain language, low literacy)

### Takeaway
WCAG covers captions (A for pre-recorded, AA for live) but puts sign-language interpretation only at AAA, so "WCAG AA compliant" does not mean Deaf-accessible. Emergency services in the EU are moving to RTT and Total Conversation. Low-literacy realities (section 2) call for plain language, icons and sign video as first-class content.

### Cited Findings
- WCAG 2.x success criteria:
  - 1.2.2 Captions (Prerecorded): Level A.
  - 1.2.4 Captions (Live): Level AA.
  - 1.2.6 Sign Language (Prerecorded): only Level AAA.
  - Source: [W3C Understanding SC 1.2.6](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/sign-language-prerecorded.html); [W3C Understanding SC 1.2.4](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/captions-live.html)
- EN 301 549 (the EU ICT accessibility standard) references WCAG 2.1 AA for interfaces, including emergency communications — [Accessible.org](https://accessible.org/eaa-emergency-communications/)
- The EAA requires RTT and Total Conversation for functional equivalence in emergency communications from 28 June 2025 — [EENA strategies](https://eena.org/knowledge-hub/documents/strategies-for-accessible-emergency-communications)
- WFD: design of new technologies, especially those used in crises, must follow inclusive and universal design principles — [UN DESA / WFD presentation](https://social.desa.un.org/sites/default/files/inline-files/8.4%20Haralsdottir%20Presentation.pdf)
- Convo combined a visual notification system with VRS, a market signal that visual alerts are a core need — [Newswire](https://www.newswire.com/news/convo-launches-platform-for-the-deaf-integrating-visual)

### Inferences
Design rules for the app:
- Never use sound as the only signal. Use vibration patterns, a screen or camera flash, and full-screen visual alerts for incoming video or interpreter calls.
- Give video a large viewport, front camera and a stable phone stand. A minimum resolution and frame rate are needed for handshapes and facial grammar. Facial expression is the top quality criterion (section 1).
- Offer plain Uzbek (Latin and Cyrillic) and Russian, short sentences, icons on every action, and sign-video help for onboarding and errors.
- Onboarding should not require reading long text or a voice call (no voice-only OTP; use SMS or Telegram codes).
- Target WCAG 2.2 AA as a floor, and treat sign-language content (AAA in WCAG) as a product requirement.

### Gaps
- Not verified: specific research on vibration and flash notification efficacy, or on designing apps for low-literacy Deaf users. No dedicated study was retrieved.
- No Uzbekistan accessibility law or standard requirements were researched (out of scope).

## 6. Ethics and privacy (video data, contributor pay, governance, replacing interpreters)

### Takeaway
Sign-language data is biometric: faces and bodies are the language. Collecting it needs explicit consent, Deaf data ownership and fair pay. Using mainly interpreter-produced data distorts the language. Deaf leadership must start at agenda-setting. Replacing human interpreters with AI is opposed by the community and by interpreting bodies, and they say VRI's unsupervised rollout already caused harm.

### Cited Findings
- Sign datasets contain recordings of people signing, which is "highly personal". Deaf community members are central stakeholders in any application built from that data — [Bragg et al., FATE Landscape of Sign Language AI Datasets, TACCESS 2021](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2021/09/3436996.pdf)
- Filters (blur, avatars) change people's willingness to participate, which is a privacy trade-off in data collection — [Bragg et al. ASSETS 2020](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/10/Exploring_Collection_of_Sign_Language_Datasets.pdf)
- Dataset bias: three interpreter-only datasets dominate continuous recognition (41 of 60 works). Interpreted TV broadcasts may not represent language as used in Deaf communities, and "fluency" is defined in ways that can exclude Deaf signers — [Desai et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.02563v1)
- Deaf-led efforts can become tokenistic and must include diverse Deaf perspectives. Hearing researchers should take support roles — [Desai et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.02563v1)
- The WFD 2025 checklist covers data ownership, community involvement, transparency and real-world performance — [WFD](https://wfdeaf.org/?p=12027)
- Sign-language users will be disproportionately affected by AI in interpreting, like speakers of other low-resource languages — [SAFE-AI Guidance press release](https://www.newswire.com/view/content/first-of-its-kind-ethical-ai-guidance-for-interpreting-released-by-22376003)
- VRI deployed in hospitals without Deaf oversight "has been extremely harmful", a cautionary precedent for AI interpreting — [3Play ACCESS 2024](https://www.3playmedia.com/access/2024-hub/deaf-safe-ai)
- Microsoft's ASL Citizen dataset is cited as community-centred curation with Deaf team members throughout — [Microsoft Research, data inequity](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=987693)

### Inferences
Concrete policies for the app:
1. **Consent**: separate opt-in for (a) service delivery and (b) research/model training. Withdrawal must be possible, and the user's videos must then be deleted.
2. Interpreted call content is never stored by default, the same confidentiality as an interpreter's.
3. Pay Deaf data contributors and Deaf reviewers at professional rates. Disclose how the data is used.
4. Set up a Deaf advisory board with real veto power, e.g. drawn from the Uzbekistan Society of the Deaf, with paid seats.
5. Hire Deaf staff, including in product roles.
6. Publish an "AI will not replace interpreters in medical, legal or emergency contexts" pledge.
7. Video of faces combined with health or legal content is highly sensitive. Check Uzbek personal-data law, including localisation rules (data stored inside the country), which was not researched here.

### Gaps
- No published standard pay rates for Deaf data contributors were found.
- No formal WFD/WASLI data-governance document was found beyond the conference checklist.

## 7. Deaf-led or co-designed companies and lessons

### Takeaway
Verifiable evidence is thin. Convo (US, Deaf-owned since 2009) is the clearest example of a Deaf-owned communications company at scale. Its model is human-interpreter-centred VRS and VRI, and it later added visual notifications. Hearing-founded avatar companies (Hand Talk) are commercially visible, but the Deaf community is reported as lukewarm about avatars, and independent evidence of co-design is lacking.

### Cited Findings
- Convo was founded by Deaf telecom experts. It is "Deaf-owned, Deaf-run and signing-centric", operates in ASL, BSL and Auslan, absorbed the UK company SignLive, and made the Forbes Accessibility 200 (2026) — [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/convo-communications); [Convo history of SignLive](https://info.convo.io/uk/help/history-of-signlive); [Convo core values](https://info.convo.io/uk/help/convos-focus-and-our-core-values); [Convo Forbes](https://www.convo.io/post/convo-on-the-2026-forbes-accessibility-200)
- The Deaf-ownership framing is a market differentiator analysed in academic business-communication literature — [SAGE 2024](https://https-sage-cnpereading-com-443.webvpn1.xju.edu.cn/doi/10.1177/23294906241288829)
- Hand Talk describes the creation of its avatars, and the diversity of the Maya avatar, as research-driven. No independent Deaf evaluation was found — [Hand Talk](https://www.handtalk.me/en/blog/how-were-hugo-and-maya-created/)
- The Cboard model is open source, NGO- and UNICEF-funded, and community-translated, which reached about 44 languages — [UNICEF](https://www.unicef.org/innovation/stories/giving-every-child-voice-aac-technology)

### Inferences
Lessons for an Uzbekistan startup:
- Deaf ownership and leadership is a trust asset and a commercial differentiator.
- The commercially proven model is human interpreting delivered through technology: VRS/VRI paid by government, employers or institutions, not by Deaf individuals.
- AI and avatars come later and are scoped to low-stakes content.
- Open-source plus NGO funding (Cboard's route) suits the AAC component.
- Paying customers are likely institutions: hospitals, banks, government service centres (e.g. Uzbekistan's public service centres), taxi platforms, employers. Deaf end users should pay little or nothing, given the documented cost burden of video.

### Gaps
- Not researched or verified: other Deaf-led startups (e.g. GoSign, and Signapse's Deaf staff ratio) and India's "Signing Hands" / "Hear2Read". The Convo and Deaf-startup search returned only Convo.
- No failure post-mortems of Deaf-tech startups were found. Their lessons would be valuable but are unsourced here.
- No data was found on business models or unit economics (who pays for VRI in middle-income countries).
